# Web 360 — the record-centred web app (W01–W15)

Status: building · 2026-08-15 · owner: web + api session

Fifteen screens were handed over as finished visual design (W01–W15). This
file is the data model and API contract they imply. The visual system is NOT
redesigned here — it is `design.md` (Pattadar Bloom · Material 3-guided) applied to app
surfaces: warm dark paper, amber accent, JetBrains Mono eyebrows and numerals,
hairline rules, no shadows.

## What changed conceptually

The old app was **record-type first** (Passbooks / Land & Properties /
Documents). The new one is **record first**: one property — parcel or built —
opens a 360 with six hangers, and everything else in the product is a lens
onto those same rows.

| Old | New | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land & Properties (tabbed) | **Properties** — one list, faceted | A khata and a flat are both "a thing I own" |
| Documents | **Papers** — eight shelves | A vault has shelves, not a table |
| — | **Shared with me** | Someone else's kit, read-only, never in your totals |
| — | **Waiting on you** | Ordered work that has come back for your decision |
| — | **Services** | Orders you place against a record |
| record → flat detail | record → **Papers · Features · People · Services · Money · History** | The six questions asked of a piece of land |

Two invariants the screens assert repeatedly, and the model must enforce:

1. **A shared kit is not a holding.** `shared_kits` is keyed by
   `recipient_user_id` and never joined into portfolio aggregates. W09:
   *"Nothing here counts toward your 44.82 acres."*
2. **One file, filed once, referenced many times.** A photo is the feature's
   condition, the order's evidence and one of the parcel's 31 — the same row,
   three lenses. W14 says this in words; the schema must not copy rows.

## W04 on real ground

The two map screens shipped as inline SVG in a normalised 0..1 space — a
deliberate placeholder, because inventing satellite imagery under records that
have never been surveyed is a lie the rest of the product does not tell. W04
now draws on a real basemap wherever the record earns one.

**Where the shape comes from.** `parcels.boundary` / `properties.boundary` —
corner-ordered `"lat,lng;lat,lng;…"`, the FMB point table's own convention.
The column is not new (`main.py`, with `updateParcelBoundary` and an
iOS-facing contract); until now nothing read it. `web360.py` parses it with
`_ring()` and exposes it as `BoundaryView.ring` / `MapRecord.ring`, flat
`[lat,lon,…]` like `shape` beside it. Fewer than three corners returns `[]`,
and a single malformed corner drops the whole ring — three sides of a parcel
is worse than none.

**What each state means.** A record with a ring gets Leaflet over
OpenStreetMap (street) and Esri World Imagery (satellite), both key-free, and
the `FMB sketch | Satellite | Both` control becomes live: the sheet over the
village's roads, the bare ground, or the two compared. A record without one
keeps the groundless sketch, the control is disabled, and the panel says why.

**It draws, it never measures.** Every figure stays server-side off the
projected corner table. Recomputing Field No. 01 from its own exported
five-decimal ring gives 60.20 ac against the table's 59.90 — 0.30 ac of
rounding, enough to move the §7 verdict from 0.17% to 0.33%, and on a smaller
parcel enough to flip it. `test_area_must_not_be_recomputed_from_the_exported_ring`
holds that line, and `area_ac` / `perimeter_m` travel inside the exported
GeoJSON because they cannot be recovered from it.

**Handing off.** `mapsLink()` in `packages/core/src/land/geo.ts` builds the
device's own map: `maps.apple.com` (a universal link, so it opens the Maps app
on iPhone, iPad and Mac and stays a web page everywhere else), `geo:` on
Android so the choice of app stays the owner's, and OpenStreetMap otherwise.
No maps app takes a boundary, so the pin is `ringCentroid()` — area-weighted,
because averaging corners puts the pin outside an L-shaped field. The button
names the app that will actually open.

**Still a placeholder:** W06 find-by-map. It draws every record at once in one
shared 0..1 space, so going real needs the screen's layout reworked (its
`<main>` is a hard `24rem` rail with no media query) rather than a component
swapped. `MapRecord.ring` is already served for when it is — and W02's map view
below now serves it, on a screen that already owns the filter.

## What a card shows of itself

A property card used to open with a tinted gradient and the illustration for
its classification. That drawing is the same drawing on every agricultural
parcel in the account: decoration, not information. A card now shows, in order:

1. **The photograph the owner took** — `RecordCard.coverFileRef`.
2. **The ground the record sits on** — a real satellite thumbnail with the
   surveyed boundary drawn on it, or a pin.
3. **The illustration**, when it knows neither.

The photo wins because it is the only one of the three the OWNER made. The map
beats the illustration because it is about THIS record.

**The chain is nested, not sequential.** The map is the photo's `fallback`,
because a cover reference is not a promise of bytes — the seed writes 167 photo
rows with an empty `file_ref`, and a session can expire in a field. The icon
stays in the markup underneath the whole stack and is simply painted over, so
no arrangement of failures — no bytes, no session, no tiles, no location —
leaves a card blank.

**No Leaflet on the cards.** Measured in the real box: forty `still`
MapCanvases cost 949 DOM nodes, 80 ResizeObservers, 40 window resize listeners
and 107 tile requests, against 235 nodes and 39 tiles for the same forty
pictures built from tile arithmetic — and the Leaflet figure is a floor,
excluding the geocoding cascade a card would need for a record with no ring. A
card thumbnail is not a map; it is a picture of one. `MapThumb` positions
`<img>` tiles by hand and draws the ring as one inline SVG path.

- **`packages/core/src/land/tiles.ts`** carries the Web Mercator arithmetic —
  `lonLatToPixel`, `boundsZoom`, `snapZoom`. The risk it carries is being a
  SECOND projection in a repo that already has Leaflet's, so `geo-tests.ts`
  pins it against Leaflet's own numbers to the sub-pixel. That test is the
  reason the file is allowed to exist.
- **The zoom snaps to a four-rung ladder (14–17).** Neighbouring parcels sit on
  the same tiles but only share a REQUEST when they share a zoom: on the demo
  account 26 tile elements collapse to 21 distinct URLs, and per-card
  `fitBounds` would share none of them. One fixed zoom would share the most and
  is still wrong — the founder's largest parcel is 355 m across and overflows
  the band at z16.
- **Esri imagery, not OpenStreetMap**, and that is a finding rather than a
  taste. Fetched over the founder's own Chintagunta parcel, the OSM tile at z16
  is **103 bytes and 100% one colour** — OSM's land fill. Rural Andhra is not
  drawn. A street-tile grid here would be a beige rectangle forty times over.
- **The ring wears the same `w-ring` class it wears on the real map**, which is
  how the tokens and the light/dark toggle follow it onto a card. An unsurveyed
  record gets a pin and never a little square: it has a POSITION and not an
  extent.
- **A scrim, once there is a photograph under the controls.** The status pills,
  the select box and the kebab were drawn for a flat tinted gradient; over real
  ground they are drawn for nothing in particular, since bare soil at midday is
  near-white and a field in shade is near-black. Same problem the on-map chips
  have — but a row of pills cannot carry its own ground without looking like a
  toolbar, so the ground goes under all of them at once.

**The photo path needs a Bearer token, so `<img src>` cannot load one.** The
bytes come from `/api/gateway/storage/files/{id}/content` behind
`require_auth`; `PhotoImg` fetches with the token and hands over a blob URL.
Two consequences worth writing down:

- The e2e suite could not regress image rendering **at all** — 0 of 167 seeded
  photos have bytes. The test now files a photo through `addPhoto` and serves
  its bytes with `page.route`, which is the only way this suite can see an
  `<img>` render.
- There is no stored thumbnail: `?thumb=` decoded the whole original with
  Pillow and re-encoded it on **every** request, and the response carried no
  ETag, no Cache-Control and no Last-Modified. Forty cards, then a click into a
  record and back, was forty more full decodes. The gateway now tags the
  response over the source bytes **and** the transform asked for, and answers
  304 *above* the decode. `services/gateway/tests/test_content_cache.py`.

**`RecordCard` gained `lat`, `lon`, `ring` and `coverFileRef`,** reversing a
decision recorded here — that a boundary should not go down the wire for the
fifteen screens that draw no map. The grid is now one of the screens that draws
a map, and it is the busiest in the app. A ring is 8–15 corners, so forty of
them is ~8 KB on a response already carrying forty titles, owners, khatas and
tag lists; a second round trip to fetch it is the larger number. The map view
below dropped its `mapRecords` join as a result — one query, one source of
truth.

## W02's third view — everything you own, on one map

The Properties switcher had two positions, Grid and List. It has three: **Map**,
drawing the records the filter left on real ground.

It draws `cards` — the FILTERED list — and not the account. The rail, the search
box and the map are then three views of one answer; a map that ignored the rail
beside it would be a fourth opinion on screen at the same time. It inherits the
bulk bar for free, which was never view-gated.

**A sibling component, not a prop on MapCanvas.** MapCanvas answers "where is
THIS record and what shape is it", and about half its props are structurally
singular: `sideLabels` index into one ring, `dimOutside` punches one hole in a
world polygon, and both the reveal and the ambient light `querySelector` the
first `path.w-ring`. A multi-record mode inside it would not be a prop, it would
be a second component sharing a file. VillageCanvas is no better a fit: it has no
pin at all, keys its shapes on a plot number that repeats across villages, and is
imagery-only. So `PortfolioCanvas`, behind the same lazy seam — load-bearing
here rather than tidy, because Properties is the busiest screen in the app and it
lands in the grid.

**SVG, not canvas.** VillageCanvas renders to canvas because Munagapadu is 2,729
plots. A portfolio is eight to forty, which is few enough to keep the DOM — and
keeping it is what lets every colour come from a token that follows the light and
dark schemes instead of a hex literal frozen into a canvas fill. Colour is
status, the same encoding the cards use.

**Two kinds of thing on it, and they are drawn differently on purpose.** A
surveyed record is its outline. An unsurveyed one is a pin, and a pin and not a
little square: that record has a POSITION and not an extent, and drawing it as a
shape would claim a boundary it does not have.

**Null island.** `geo_point` is a pin somebody dropped; a boundary is a survey,
and a record can easily have the second and not the first — five of the eight
parcels in the founder's own account do. The pin parser answers `0,0` for an
empty column, which is a real place in the Gulf of Guinea, so half a portfolio
would have plotted in the Atlantic. Two changes, one on each side:

- Server: `_located(geo, ring)` falls back to the average of the ring's corners,
  which for the convex shapes a parcel is lands inside the parcel. A pin still
  outranks it — the pin is where a person said the record is, and a person's
  answer outranks arithmetic. `services/api/tests/test_located.py`.
- Client: `isLocated()` treats a position within 0.01° of the origin as *no
  position*. Anything under a thousand kilometres of null island is not a land
  record in Andhra Pradesh, so nothing real is excluded. It lives in its own
  Leaflet-free module because the caption and the card thumbnails need the same
  rule, and importing it from the canvas would pull Leaflet in eagerly.

**What is missing takes up no space.** A map is the one view that can look
complete while quietly leaving records out, so the count is always stated in
words under it — "9 records drawn — 1 from a survey, 8 from a pin" — and any
record that is neither surveyed nor pinned is named.

**Names are measured, not estimated.** Labels are an overlay rather than N
Leaflet markers, because a label that would land on one already placed is
dropped, and deciding that needs every label's box at once. The first pass
guessed a width from the character count and let two survey numbers overlap —
two numbers written over each other read as a third that does not exist. Each
label is now appended, measured, and taken away again if it landed on a
neighbour.

**It fits.** The panel is sized from the VIEWPORT
(`clamp(22rem, calc(100vh - 20rem), 44rem)`), the same idiom every other map
surface here uses, rather than flex-filling its column — see `.split > .plot.live`
for what a live map that grows to match the list beside it does to its own
subject. `/app/properties` is the first map-bearing route in this suite under a
sideways-scroll gate; the existing responsive test walks it in grid view, where
no map mounts, so it had never checked one. There are now three that do, at
1512 / 900 / 390.

## W07 writes back

The features grid shipped able to file a name and nothing else. A feature added
through it arrived as category `other`, icon `feature`, condition empty and
state `good` — a card with a bare green dot and no way, anywhere in the web app,
to say what the thing was or that it had stopped working. Three things fix it,
and the first two belong to the API rather than to the browser.

**The name decides the kind.** `_classify_feature` in `web360.py` maps a label
to `(category, icon)` — "Bore" is water and draws a bore, "Compound wall" is a
structure and not a well. It runs on `addFeature` when the caller gives no
category, and again on `updateFeature` when the label changes, so the chip row,
the typed-in name and anything the phone files later cannot classify the same
word three different ways. `('other', 'feature')` is the honest miss, and the
Other chip is where those collect to be corrected.

**Omitted and empty are different instructions.** `updateFeature` takes every
field as nullable: absent leaves the column alone, `""` clears it. Under the old
non-empty-wins rule a spec or a note could never be deleted once typed — the
delete appeared to work until the page was reloaded. The name is the exception:
an empty label is refused, because a feature nobody can find again is worse
than a badly named one.

**Not checked is a state.** A feature filed from the field is `unknown`, not
`good`. Nobody has looked at it, and painting the dot green on the strength of
a name being typed is an assurance the app invented for itself. It sorts above
`good` and below `warn` — the next thing to look at, not the last — and carries
its own chip so "never inspected" is never counted as "broken".

Worst-first is computed in the resolver now, not read off the `sort` column.
`sort` is the order things were filed in, and a bore that failed this morning
is filed last.

## Village maps

Everywhere else you start from a record and ask where it is. `/app/villages`
starts from the ground: the survey department's own shape file for a village,
every plot in it, and from a plot you can add the survey number and mapped
boundary to Properties or hand the boundary to a record that never had one.

**Two ways in, one parser.** `services/api/src/villagemap.py` does the reading.
`scripts/village-map-import.py` uses it to build the shipped maps from
`data/vm/` into `apps/web/public/vm/`, which Vite serves statically; the API's
`POST /village-maps` uses it for files uploaded through the page. A village
built at the desk and the same village uploaded from a laptop cannot come out
different, which is the only reason to have one module rather than two.

**The plot number is often not on the plot.** The department's older exports
(Komarolu, Podili) carry the polygons in one file and the numbers in another,
as label points floating over them — 219 shapes all called "Burada Palem", and
"find plot 74" finds nothing. The numbers are recovered by asking which polygon
each label stands in. Pairing by position in the file is tempting and wrong: it
agrees with containment for 726 of Hanumanthunipadu's 731 plots, and those five
would be five plots wearing their neighbour's number. The leftovers — a plot
bent into an L has its own centre outside itself — take the nearest unclaimed
label within their own bounding box grown by a fifth. Across eight villages
that yields 7,946 plots, no duplicate number anywhere, and three shapes dropped
for having no number at all.

**The village's name is not reliably anywhere.** One file is called
`Ramayanakadnrika`, its folder says `Ramayanakadrika`, and the shapefile path
it came from says `Ramayanam Kandrika`. A working copy is called `asw.kml`.
Every name in the file is a candidate — Windows paths cut to their last
segment, departmental codes and job titles ("LPM SHAPE FILE") stripped — and
the longest survivor wins, which picks the spelled-out name over the run
together or cut short one.

**Duplicates.** Files are grouped by `villageKey` and only the best set is
written: most plots numbered, then fewest left nameless, then the smaller
source. That is what rejected `asw.kml`, a 806 KB Google Earth save of a
village whose department export is 30 KB — it carried a hand-drawn shape and a
measurement, and both scored as plots until "Untitled anything" was ruled out
as a plot number.

**Drawing the mesh.** The plots are canvas polygons, which cannot read the
stylesheet, so their colour is one of the few values written into
`VillageCanvas.tsx`. On imagery it is white — a hairline of `--w-ink-3` at 0.55
opacity, which is what this drew first, is the exact grey-brown of a ploughed
field in Prakasam, and 89 plots over Esri's tiles came out as a photograph of
farmland with nothing on it. On the drawn map it is the ink token. Both are
redrawn when the basemap changes, which `/app/villages` now switches with the
same one-button toggle the record map has.

Line width is two buckets on zoom. Framed on the whole of Munagapadu — 2,729
plots across 6 km — a 1 px outline per plot is wider than the gaps between
them and the town core fills in solid, so the mesh thins to 0.45 px below z14
and comes back at the zoom where somebody is looking for their own plot.

The regression test is pixel-level (`crud-360.spec.ts`), and it counts two
things: how much of the canvas is lit, and how much of that is light enough to
carry over a photograph. Only the second would have failed on the original bug
— the plots were always drawn, and every other kind of assertion passed while
the screen showed nothing.

**The landing is the mandal.** With no village chosen the map draws every
village on record — its real outline, its name, its plot count and its extent —
because the question there is "which village", and eight shapes on one map
answer it better than eight names in a list. The outlines are the real edges,
not a hull: all eight are 77 KB against 2.2 MB of plots, so there is no reason
to approximate. `scripts/village-map-import.py` writes them to
`apps/web/public/vm/overview.json` and the API stores the same for anything
uploaded, computed by the same `outer_edges` — the Python twin of the
TypeScript one, and verified to agree segment for segment on all eight
villages (232, 251, 277, 232, 232, 180, 309, 155).

A village needs a shape to be clicked, and an outline is a heap of loose
segments, so a convex hull of those segments is the hit target — never drawn,
because it fills every bay the real edge cuts into. Village names use the same
ladder the plot labels do (full label, name alone, nothing): these villages are
25 km apart north to south and a few hundred metres apart in the middle, so
zoomed out to hold all of them the middle ones would sit on top of each other.
Whatever the map can fit, the panel beside it lists every village.

**The screen is the map.** `/app/villages` is a map with one inspector column
beside it (24.75rem), not a panel in a page: a cadastre is read by moving
around it, so every control that changes what is shown sits ON the map — the
four modes and the numbers/measure toggles top left, the village and zoom
readout in the middle, and the survey/plot finder top right. The inspector is
one column because what it answers is one train of thought: which of these
thousands is mine, how big is it, and what touches it.

**Find one plot; do not render the village twice.** There is no permanent
“All plots” column. It repeated hundreds or thousands of numbers already drawn
on the map, pushed the selected plot below a second scroll, and gave keyboard
users a different substring search from the exact finder on the map. The one
finder is a combobox over the map: normalised survey-number prefixes produce at
most eight compact suggestions, exact matches rank first, and choosing one
moves the map and opens the same selected-plot inspector as clicking its shape.
Typing `1`, for example, offers plot numbers beginning with `1`; it does not
list every number merely containing that digit. A map click writes the chosen
number back into the finder, so pointer and keyboard selection stay one state.

Adding an unowned mapped plot creates its land-parcel record and saves the
shape-file boundary before navigating. Only after both writes succeed does the
screen open `/app/properties`, where the newest parcel is visible; a refused
boundary leaves the user on the plot and retries the boundary alone, never a
second record.

**Four modes.** *Satellite* is Esri imagery with white plot outlines.
*Street map* is the labelled OpenStreetMap ground layer. *Plot size* shades every plot by extent — a magnitude, so one hue light to
dark (the validated blue ramp, ordered dim-to-bright because it is read
against a photograph rather than paper), with a legend carrying live per-band
counts. Blue and not the accent, so a shaded plot is never mistaken for the
selected one. *Boundaries* drops the imagery entirely and draws the cadastre on
the panel's own surface — that is the mode, not a fallback. A dark street layer
sat there first; CARTO now answers every tile with API KEY REQUIRED stamped
across it.

Each chip is a switch, not a radio button: clicking the one that is already on
turns it off. Off for the imagery is the bare cadastre — which is what
Boundaries is — and off for anything else is back to the imagery, where the
screen starts.

**Measuring requires satellite ground.** A tape over the bare cadastre or a
size-colour layer measures a drawing, not the bund, tree line or track someone
is checking in the field. “Measure on satellite” therefore remembers the
current display mode, switches to Satellite, and locks all four layer controls
for as long as the tape is out. The active Satellite chip stays visibly on and
the measure panel states the dependency; Numbers remains independent. Putting
the tape away or pressing Escape clears its points and restores the exact map
mode the reader had chosen before measuring, so the necessary override never
becomes a hidden preference change.

The tape itself is drawn the way a boundary is drawn on the record map, and
with the same two labels, because they are the same two facts: a letter at
every corner (A, B, C — letters and not numbers, since a map covered in numbers
cannot afford a "3" beside a "136 m") and a length on every side, the closing
one included once three points make a shape. Each length sits against its own
edge, pushed along that edge's normal away from the middle of the shape and
walked further out if a corner disc is in the way — the offsets are screen
space, so they are recomputed on every zoom and pan.

**Three levels, and the head says which one.** The mandal (`Maps`, every
village), a village in it (`MADALAVARIPALEM`, its plots), and one parcel of
that village with a tool open on it (`Plot 105`). Each level names the one
above it in the eyebrow and carries a single button back up — "All villages"
from a village, "Back to MADALAVARIPALEM" from a parcel. While the fence
calculator has the stage the inspector column stands down entirely: it was
answering the question the calculator's own bar answers, in the same words, and
the tool would rather have the width.

**Fencing is a tool, not a card.** `FenceStudio` takes the map's place rather
than sitting in the inspector: fencing is a job you do to a SHAPE, and its
questions are about the ground — which sides, where the gate goes, how far
apart the posts stand. In a 24-rem column the thing being priced was never in
view. In the studio the boundary fills the screen with its corners lettered and
its sides dimensioned (MapCanvas, the same component the record map uses, so
side-clicking and corner letters come for nothing), and the panel sits on
whichever hand suits the plot.

Three steps — what is being fenced, what with, and what it comes to — all on
one panel rather than behind Next buttons. A wizard hides the effect of the
answer just given, and the whole value here is watching the total move. Step 1
is the sides, each with the letters it wears on the map, each droppable:
**most fences go round part of a boundary, not all of it**, and dropping a side
also opens the run, so the corner count follows. A gate is modelled as what it
is — a hole in the fence: no wire across it, two posts either side. Wire is
bought in rolls, so the estimate states the rolls to order and not only the
metres.

It prices whatever ring is in front of you — the shape just measured if the
tape is out, otherwise the selected plot — and it is side-aware, which is the
whole point.

**An estimate that cannot leave the screen is arithmetic, not a tool.** It
leaves two ways.

*Printed*, for the supplier. What Print used to put on paper was the FORM: the
rail, the input boxes, and a panel cut off two fields above the answer — the
one artefact meant to leave the app, and the answer was not on it. (Two causes:
the print rules hid `.rail`, and the rail is `.nav`, so a class that does not
exist hid nothing; and the shell is a two-column grid, where a `display: none`
child still leaves its track, squeezing the sheet into 340 px down the left.)
Now `.fs-sheet` is the only thing on the page: black on white, the plot drawn
to scale with its corners lettered and its sides dimensioned in inline SVG —
because a satellite photograph prints as a grey rectangle and states no
lengths — then the line, the build, what to buy, the money and the footnote.

*Raised as work on the record*, for whoever is going to build it: a
`createRequest` of kind `fencing` carrying the whole bill as its message, which
lands in Services at "Placed". Deliberately a REQUEST and not an expense or a
feature — the fence does not exist yet and the money has not been spent, and
writing either of those down would put a thing on the land that is not there.
It appears only when the plot IS one of the account's records; when it is not,
the panel says the plot has to be filed first rather than offering a button
with nothing to hang off.

The build and the rates are kept in `localStorage` between visits. A post costs
what it costs wherever you buy it, and retyping four figures every time you
price a different plot is what stops a tool being used. `fenceEstimate` in core (two older
screens still call it) says posts = perimeter ÷ spacing; that is not what
anybody buys. A fence turns at its corners, so there is a post AT every corner
whether or not the spacing lands one there, and a run between two corners
cannot be spanned by a stride that ignores them. On 100/80/100/80 m at 3 m the
blunt sum says 120 posts and the sides say 122 — and the two extra are the ones
holding the fence up. `fencePlan()` is the side-aware one, with
`scripts/fence-tests.ts` behind it.

Spacing is a maximum, not a stride: each side is divided into whole intervals
no longer than it, so 100 m at 3 m is 34 intervals of 2.94 m rather than 33 of
3 m and a 1 m orphan. Wire is the perimeter once per STRAND. Costs are per post
and per metre of wire, the way a fencing quote is itemised, and both are left
empty until the owner fills them in — a rate is local and this screen has no
business guessing one. The total is labelled Materials, with labour, gates,
corner bracing and cartage named as not being in it.

One wording trap worth keeping: the tape reports what was WALKED and the fence
goes all the way round, so on a closed shape the two figures differ by the
closing side. The card says so rather than leaving two numbers to disagree in
silence.

**A plot answers for itself.** Selecting one gives its number, its extent in
all three units a paper record uses (`4.893 acres` / `4 Acres 35.72 Guntas` /
`1.980 ha`), whether that figure is off the sheet or measured off the polygon,
its chaltha, its centroid, and the plots adjoining it. The owner, the passbook
and the papers come from THIS ACCOUNT'S records where one matches the plot —
matched on the folded village name and the number in the record's title — and
where none does the panel says "Not one of your records" rather than inventing
a name for somebody's field. The shape file has no owner in it, and no amount
of screen design can put one there.

**Adjoining plots.** The question a village map is opened with is "who owns the
one next to mine", and it is not answered by comparing corner lists.
Neighbouring parcels in a cadastral export rarely share a whole edge: one long
boundary faces two or three shorter ones across it, and the corners on either
side are metres apart. Matching identical edges — or edge midpoints — reports a
mean of 1.3 neighbours per plot where the truth is nearer six.

So edges are matched as collinear overlapping segments. Every polygon edge is
reduced to a direction folded to [0°, 180°) (a line has no arrow), a signed
perpendicular offset, and its extent along its own direction; edges are
bucketed by whole-degree angle and sorted by offset, so a candidate's matches
are a contiguous window found by binary search rather than a scan of thousands.
Two plots adjoin when offsets agree within 2e-6° (~0.2 m), directions are
parallel within `|dot| > 0.999`, and the segments overlap by more than 6e-5°
(~6 m) — which excludes plots that only meet at a corner. Across the eight
villages that gives a mean of 4.7–5.4 neighbours, one isolated plot in 7,946,
and 1–12 ms for villages up to 2,729 plots. **If you port this, assert the mean
lands near 5 — that is the regression test.**

The same machinery draws the village's own edge: an edge covered along less
than half its run by the plots on the other side is facing open ground. It is
ragged where the cadastre is ragged, and it should be — a sliver of unclaimed
land inside a village is a real gap in the sheet, and drawing a tidy ring
around it would be inventing a boundary.

**The number goes on the plot.** Zoomed in, each plot carries its own number
and, under it, its extent — written in the middle of the shape. The trigger is
the plot's size in pixels, not a zoom level: at any one zoom these run from a
tenth of an acre to six hundred, so a threshold would either bury the small
plots or leave the big ones bare. Below about 26 px across, nothing is written.

Stacked, not side by side. "839 · 4.893 ac" on one line needs ~74 px, and a
plot at the zoom where a village is readable is nearer 40. Under the number the
widest line is the figure alone. Each rung of the ladder is narrower than the
last: both lines, the figure without its unit, the number alone, nothing.

**The extent has a floor of its own: 200 m on the scale bar.** Above that the
number is written and the acres are not — at 300 m a village is 155 labels and
you are looking for where your land *is*, not how big it is; the acres are 60%
more label to fit in a frame that already cannot hold the numbers. The rule is
written in the units on the screen rather than a zoom level, because a zoom
level is a different distance in every district: `scaleBar()` repeats Leaflet's
own arithmetic (ground distance across 100 px, rounded down to the largest 1,
2, 3 or 5 × 10ⁿ that fits) and the gate is `<= 200`. Measured on Marripalem:
500 m → 0 of 15 labels with acres, 300 m → 0 of 155, 200 m → 145 of 196,
100 m → 74 of 78, 50 m → 17 of 17.

Two rules keep it readable. The label sits at the middle of the bounding box
unless that point falls outside the plot — an L-shaped plot has its centre in
somebody else's field — in which case it moves to the middle of the widest span
of the plot along that line. And labels are placed largest-plot-first, with any
that would collide dropped to number-only and then dropped entirely: a number
written over another number answers neither question. Only what is in view is
built, since a village is thousands of plots and every label is a DOM node.

Extents are printed as the department wrote them ("5.0 ac" beside "4.893 ac"),
not rounded to look tidy. The villages whose exports carry no extent — the
label-matched ones — show the number alone rather than a figure nobody issued.

**Uploaded maps shadow shipped ones.** They live in `village_maps`, keyed by
the folded village name, so uploading a village twice replaces it rather than
stacking a copy, and re-uploading is how a bad map is corrected. Reads are
deliberately not scoped to the uploader: a revenue village's shape file is the
department's published record of the ground, and nothing in it belongs to
whoever happened to have the KMZ. The browser merges the two manifests
(`/vm/index.json` and `GET /village-maps`) with the upload winning, in
`apps/web/src/w360/villageIndex.ts` — the one place that knows the difference.

## W04's header, and what belongs on the map

The record map's header grew to seven buttons in a row — there is a test named
"seven actions wrap instead of running off the page", which is the row telling
on itself. They were never seven of a kind:

- two chose what you were LOOKING at (Satellite, Village map),
- three CHANGED THE RECORD (Move the pin, Draw boundary, and "Add a mark",
  which was buried in a card's aside on the far side of the screen),
- and the rest were record-level errands.

So they went where they belong. The layers are chips on the map, the writing
tools are a panel on the map under the heading **Changes the record**, and the
header keeps only `Order a survey`, the primary `Upload boundary from KML`, and a
`···`. Measure is a chip and NOT in that panel: it changes what you see and
nothing else, and a group headed "changes the record" has to be true of every
row in it.

Three things the move taught, all of them worth keeping:

- **Armed, the panel collapses to the armed tool.** It stands on the map, and
  the next thing to happen is a click on the map. A full menu over the corner
  you meant to place is the tool getting in the way of itself.
- **What the map SAYS goes to the bottom.** The instruction, the draft bar and
  the location error were stacked under the tools, and in draw mode that column
  grew tall enough to swallow a corner — the test that files four corners
  quietly got three.
- **The tape outranks the other two chips.** Measuring is about this parcel and
  dims everything else; the village map is about the two thousand around it.
  See the next section — the rule is the same one the Maps page keeps, and it
  took two attempts to get right.

**One rule for both maps: the tape brings the ground with it.** A side checked
against a road map is checked against a drawing. The reason to walk a length off
on either of these screens is to hold it against something real — the bund, the
tree line, the track — and none of that is in OpenStreetMap. So while Measure is
out it outranks the other two chips: the imagery is **held on** and the village
map is **out of reach**. Measure is therefore never itself disabled; it is the
one control that releases the other two.

The Maps page had this from the start (`beforeTape`, VillageMaps). The record map
took two attempts:

- The first made Measure and the village map plain *alternatives* — each click
  turning the other off. That was a workaround for a real problem, not the rule:
  measuring is on by default here, so disabling the chip outright would have had
  it born disabled.
- The second is the rule, with the default faced honestly. The record map lands
  taped, so it lands on imagery with two chips greyed, and the one control that
  frees them is the lit chip beside them. A greyed control with no reason next to
  it reads as a broken one, so the reason is on screen — not only in a tooltip:
  *"The imagery stays on while you measure, and the village map waits."*

Two details that are load-bearing rather than tidy:

- **The rule is keyed on `taped = measuring && surveyed`, never on `measuring`.**
  `measuring` is true on an *unsurveyed* record too — it simply has nothing to
  measure, so no Measure chip is drawn. Keyed on `measuring` alone the rule would
  lock the imagery on and the village map out on exactly the records that need
  the village map most, with no control anywhere to undo it. A tape you cannot
  put away is not a tool, it is a trap. There is a test named "an unsurveyed
  record is never taped into a corner".
- **There is no before-the-tape ref here, unlike the Maps page.** `satellite`
  holds what the OWNER asked for and is never written to by the tape; the tape
  only outranks it while it is out, through
  `basemap = satellite || taped ? 'satellite' : 'street'`. Putting the tape away
  restores their choice because their choice was never lost.

The two greyed chips are greyed for different reasons and must not look the same.
Satellite is HELD ON — it states a true fact about the screen, so it stays fully
legible and only loses its pointer. Village map is OUT OF REACH — it states
nothing, so it recedes to 50%. One opacity for both was the first attempt, and a
dimmed orange chip over imagery read as a rendering fault rather than a lock.

**Dimming outside.** While the tape is out, everything outside the boundary is
masked — a world-sized polygon with the parcel punched out of it as a hole,
`fill-rule: evenodd`. It needs `fill-opacity` declared in CSS: Leaflet writes
`fill-opacity="0.2"` as a presentation attribute, and a presentation attribute
only loses to a property the stylesheet actually declares, so a 42% mask
arrives at 8% and the dim is invisible. That is the second time this exact trap
has been paid for here — see `.w-mark`.

**The ambient light.** Landing on a boundary, the line lights up: a scatter of
fine points along the whole perimeter, and one soft head that travels it once
and goes out. It is the parcel saying hello — a car's headliner, not a loading
indicator, and it is over before anyone would think to wait for it.

Two clones of the ring's own `path`, inserted beside it and removed at 3.6s, so
they trace the parcel exactly, cost no geometry, and leave nothing behind. Both
carry `pathLength="1"`, which makes every dash figure in the stylesheet a
*fraction of this parcel's perimeter*: without it the animation is a function of
size, and a smallholding flickers while an estate crawls.

It is deliberately **not** hung off the once-ever reveal it sits beside, though
it was at first. That reveal is a first-run explanation — it says *this line is
your boundary*, and saying it twice would be talking down to someone. A greeting
is the opposite: one you only get once is one you stop noticing. So the reveal
stays behind `w360-boundary-intro` in localStorage and the light plays on every
landing, once, keyed on the ring appearing — not on a basemap toggle, and not at
all under `prefers-reduced-motion`.

## The overflow

The record map grew to seven buttons in a row — there is a test named "seven
actions wrap instead of running off the page", which is the row telling on
itself. They are not seven of a kind: three are TOOLS you use on the map
(Measurements, Move the pin, Draw boundary), two are LAYERS (Satellite, Village
map), and the rest are one-off errands.

So the errands went behind a `···`: Export GeoJSON, Open in <the device's own
maps app>, and Print this map. The primary is `Upload boundary from KML` — on
a record with no boundary that is the thing actually needed, and it is the same
action the empty FMB card offers.

It was called `File the FMB sheet` until the label was held against the picker
it opens. That picker accepts a KML or a GeoJSON and nothing else, so an owner
holding the sheet in the form the survey office issues it — a scan, a
photograph — opened the one primary on the screen and found their own document
greyed out. A control is named for what it takes. The paper goes on the
record's Papers tab, and the empty FMB card now says so; this button is for
the corners.

Two details worth keeping:

- **The maps hand-off is still a link.** Inside a menu its ARIA role is
  `menuitem`, but the element is an `<a href>` — a button calling
  `window.open` cannot be middle-clicked, copied, or opened in a new tab, and
  is what a popup blocker stops. `MenuItem` grew an optional `href` for this.
- **The two arrows point opposite ways.** Upload is an arrow up, export is an
  arrow down. It is the one place in the app where an icon can say the exact
  opposite of its label, so it is worth checking whenever these move.

`MenuItem` also grew an optional `icon`. A menu of four one-off errands reads
much faster with them; "Edit / Delete" does not need them, so both are optional
and the existing callers are untouched.

## API

One namespace, `Query.web` / `Mutation.web` (`web360.py`), so the record-360
surface can grow without touching the 6.8k-line iOS-facing schema in
`main.py`. Wiring in `main.py` is three lines: import, `bind(pool, uid)`,
and the two namespace fields.

Reads: `portfolio`, `properties(filter)`, `facets`, `record(id)`,
`papers(recordId)`, `features(recordId)`, `people(recordId)`,
`money(recordId)`, `expenses(recordId, year)`, `photos(recordId)`,
`photo(id)`, `boundary(recordId)`, `mapRecords`, `vault`, `shareLinks`,
`sharedKits`, `sharedKit(id)`, `document(id)`.

Writes: `setTag` / `clearTag`, `saveExpense`, `updateCaption`, `moveMark`,
`acceptMarkPosition`, `deleteMark`, `extendShareLink`, `revokeShareLink`,
`setAppreciationRate`.

Record CRUD (W02's actions): `saveRecord(input)` creates a record when the
input has no id and partially updates it when it does — an omitted field is
left alone, never blanked. A parcel hangs off a passbook: create reuses the
user's matching `(pattadar_no, village)` passbook or makes one; an update
that changes passbook-owned fields (khata, owner, place) SPLITS a passbook
shared by several parcels before changing it, so siblings never move.
`deleteRecords(ids)` removes records and everything filed under them —
papers with their versions and live links, photos, features, people, money
rows, notes, tags and open orders — but leaves the passbook: the legacy
Passbooks screen still owns it. A paper is matched by whichever key filed it
(`record_id` for web-360 rows, `parcel_id` / `property_id` for the mobile
upload path and the vault backfill, which leave `record_id` empty), and the
`registered_documents` reading behind it goes too — otherwise init_db's vault
backfill recreates the document row at the next API restart. `archiveRecords(ids, archived)` flips an
`archived` flag on parcels/properties; archived records leave every list,
sum, map and search — including the Dashboard's "Waiting on you" deadlines,
its running-costs figure, the map insights and the "Your tags" facet, all of
which are constrained to live record ids — and the Properties rail grows an
"Archived" status facet only while any exist. Their papers are NOT excluded
from the vault total: an archived record keeps everything filed under it, and
W07 counts the vault, not the portfolio.

Two shapes to keep in mind when editing a record. A passbook can stand behind
several parcels, so an edit that changes passbook-owned fields clones it
first — and the clone must carry every column, `group_id` and `photo`
included, or the parcel silently drops out of its family group's acreage. And
a property can carry BOTH a land area and a built-up one while the card shows
only the built figure, so the drawer sends an extent only when the user
actually edited that field: re-sending the shown figure under a newly-chosen
unit would write square feet into the land column. `tagRecords(ids, tag)` and
`orderService(recordIds, kind)` back the bulk bar — the EC orders land in
`work_requests`, so Services and Assigned pick them up like any other.

## Services became tickets (W16)

Full design: **`docs/specs/2026-09-07-service-ticketing-payments.md`**.

An order could be placed and listed, and that was all. It could not be sent to
the surveyor, could not say that nine days had passed with no answer, could not
hold what came back until somebody looked at it, and could not put the sketch
onto the record when they were happy with it. W16 is the rest of that life.

**A ticket IS a `work_requests` row**, with six columns added — not a new table.
Everything that already reads that row keeps reading it: the Services list, the
Assigned rail, the four-pip Rail, `deleteRecords`, and the fencing job
`FenceStudio` raises. A parallel table would have meant every one of those
either reading two sources or showing half the work.

**`status` is the truth; `stage` is written from it.** `_STAGES` —
`Placed / Assigned / On site / Delivered` — is frozen, because it is a fine
progress bar and a useless record: it cannot say *I wrote to him and heard
nothing*, or *two of the four photos are of the wrong field*, or *I pulled the
job*. Eight statuses can, and the legal moves between them are one dict in
`services/api/src/ticketing.py`. A pair not in that dict does not happen — the
resolver refuses it and writes a `refused` event rather than repairing the
state, because a machine that quietly fixes itself cannot be reconstructed when
four and a half thousand rupees are being argued about. Rows written before W16
carry a stage and no status and are read through `status_of()`, never
backfilled: a reseed would undo a backfill.

**`ticketing.py` is pure, and the precedent is `fmb_geometry.py`** — no db, no
strawberry, no network, no clock, no entropy. `today` and `entropy` are
arguments, because this is the code that decides who gets paid and it has to be
pinnable in a test.

**Sending is real; paying is recorded.** A dispatch goes out through the same
`notify.py` seam the invite flow has used since it shipped — stub writes to
`notification_log`, credentials go live with no call-site change — and the
system does the sending, so the request can be withdrawn. The owner is never
handed a link to forward, which is what `createRequest` has always refused to
be, for the reason it always gave: a link you paste yourself cannot be revoked,
tracked or answered for. Money gets the identical seam, one step behind:
`service_payments` is a real double-entry ledger with a real hold, split,
settlement and return, and under the default `PAYMENTS_PROVIDER=stub` not one
rupee moves. No screen says paid, charged or debited while that is true, and
`ux-guards.ts` fails the build if one ever does. The word **escrow** is not used
in any new copy — Pattadar is not a licensed escrow agent, and what a gateway
offers is a withheld payout; new copy says *set aside*.

**Nothing joins the record until the owner accepts it, item by item.** A survey
that comes back with a good sketch and two photographs of the wrong field must
not force an all-or-nothing decision, or the owner accepts rubbish rather than
lose the sketch. Accepting files each kept item — paper to a shelf, photo to the
gallery, corrected outline over `parcels.boundary` with the old one kept,
feature to `land_features` — and a rejected deliverable is kept and never
deleted, because it is the evidence of what was sent. A filed photo carries
`source='order'` and `order_ref`, both true, and keeps `verified=false`,
`captured_by=''` and no coordinates: the owner typed the surveyor's name, and
the gallery renders that field as proof of who stood in the field.

Two new routes: `/app/tickets/:id` and `/app/wallet` — which is promoted out of
`UNDRAWN` and loses its permanent rail dot, because a dot that never clears
trains people to ignore the corner. **No seventh record hanger**: a ticket is
reached from a Services row the way a paper is reached from the Papers list.

## Seed

`scripts/seed-web360.py [user_id]` writes the figures shown in W01–W15. It is
idempotent: it deletes rows whose id starts `w360-`, and — only when the target
id is itself a `w360-*` demo identity — everything owned by that id, so rows
the app created through the UI don't survive into the next run.

**It seeds `w360-demo`, not the founder's id.** `shankarreddy.t` already owns 30
real parcels and one property; seeding a nine-record demo on top would bury
them. To see the demo:

```sh
.local/api-venv/bin/python scripts/seed-web360.py w360-demo
bun run --filter @pattadar/web dev:demo      # :5175, x-user-id: w360-demo
```

`./scripts/start-local.sh` is unchanged and still shows the founder's own
records at :5173 — the screens are data-driven and render either set.

### Filling every record — `scripts/seed-demo-data.py`

`seed-web360.py` authors the nine records the screens were drawn around.
Everything else a user owns arrives bare, and a bare record renders as a shell:
₹0, `0.0000° N`, "No paper here matches that". `seed-demo-data.py` walks every
parcel and property a user owns and fills the empty ones — papers, features,
people, payments, photos, boundary marks, purchase lots, a ledger, an order, a
note, tags, a wallet.

```sh
.local/api-venv/bin/python scripts/seed-demo-data.py shankarreddy.t
.local/api-venv/bin/python scripts/seed-demo-data.py --purge shankarreddy.t   # undo
```

**Removal is the point.** Everything it writes is either a row whose id starts
`demo-`, or a base field on a record that was empty, recorded in `demo_stamp`.
`--purge <user>` deletes the first and restores the second — verified: the
founder's 30 parcels come back to exactly 0 market value, 0 rows, nothing else
touched. A bare `--purge` clears every user; **always pass a user id** unless
you mean all of them.

It never overwrites a value that already existed, and skips any record that
already has data of that kind — which is why the hand-authored `w360-` set
survives it intact and the hero record still matches the mock byte for byte.

### One record, on purpose — `scripts/seed-record-features.py`

The skip-what-has-data rule leaves a gap: a record carrying one stray feature —
the usual state after trying the Add button once — can no longer be filled by
either seeder, and that is exactly the record somebody wants to look at.

```sh
.local/api-venv/bin/python scripts/seed-record-features.py 987          # by survey no
.local/api-venv/bin/python scripts/seed-record-features.py 987 --purge  # undo
```

Nineteen features across all five categories, every condition the screen can
draw (bad, warn, unknown, good), pinned at real coordinates tested against the
record's own boundary ring rather than scattered near it. Ids start `sf-`, so
the set lifts back out whole and nothing else on the record is touched. The
owner is read from the record, never from the command line: it cannot write
features into an account that does not hold the land.

### Fidelity — where the mock disagrees with itself

Per-record figures appear on two or three screens each and are seeded exactly:
Sy 214/2 is 3.24 ac / ₹72.9 L / ₹22.5 L an acre everywhere; Sy 88's lots are
₹60.00 L and ₹1.08 Cr; the vault's eight shelves are 14/18/7/5/12/3/31/2.
Totals are then **derived** from those rows rather than hard-coded, so a screen
always sums its own data. Three places where the mock's totals cannot follow
from its own line items, and the line items win:

| Worth ₹5.02 Cr | ₹5.72 Cr | The nine records it lists add to ₹5.72 Cr before any stake filter. `INVESTED ₹3.62 Cr` and `LOANS ₹42.0 L` are exact. |
| Peddapuram ₹2.46 Cr, Kothapalli ₹1.22 Cr | ₹2.77 Cr, ₹1.61 Cr | Sy 88 alone is ₹2.46 Cr and Shop 2 is also in Peddapuram. Kakinada town ₹78.4 L and Chinnapuram ₹55.8 L are exact. |

## Test gate

`tests/e2e-web360` — 175 Playwright tests over a self-contained stack: the API on
:18080 and the **built** web bundle on :5175, with `x-user-id: w360-demo`. The
founder's :8080/:8082/:5173 and their records are never touched. `globalSetup`
re-seeds, so the suite is re-runnable; it asserts on the figures that appear on
more than one screen, on console cleanliness, and on horizontal overflow at
1512/900/390px.

**`specs/harness.ts` is the front door**, and every spec imports `test` from it
rather than from Playwright: an auto fixture stubs the tile servers and the
geocoder before any test body runs. That was opt-in — a `stubTiles(page)` at
the top of the tests that opened a map — until the property cards started
drawing their own ground, which moved a map onto the landing screen and turned
"the tests that open a map" into "the tests". Leaving it opt-in cost a full run:
Chromium reports a dead `<img>` as a console error, `networkidle` never settles
with forty thumbnails in flight, three responsive tests timed out at 60s each,
and the suite went from 6 minutes to 49. A test that wants to see a tile FAIL
— the offline map notice — simply routes the pattern again; the last route
registered wins, so only the default changed.

```sh
cd tests/e2e-web360 && bunx playwright test
```
